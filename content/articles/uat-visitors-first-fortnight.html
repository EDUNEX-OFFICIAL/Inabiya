Quick answer: In the first fortnight, protect rest and feeding first. Keep visits short, one household at a time when you can, and make “no” easy to say. Guests who wash hands, skip kisses on the baby’s face, and leave when you ask are helping — not being rude.

The first two weeks at home are a soft landing after birth: night feeds, healing, learning a new rhythm, and watching a tiny person who still feels brand new. Friends and family often want to celebrate — and that love is real — but too many visitors, too soon, can leave parents depleted and babies overstimulated. This guide is practical etiquette for a gentler fortnight. It is general parenting information, not medical advice.

Why the first fortnight needs a softer door

Newborns are adjusting to light, sound, temperature, and feeding. Parents are adjusting to broken sleep, recovery, and a flood of decisions. A steady stream of guests adds talking, holding, perfume, and cleanup at the exact moment your nervous system wants quiet.

Protecting this window is not about shutting people out forever. It is about sequencing: recover, establish feeding, then widen the circle. Many families find that a short, clear plan upfront prevents awkwardness later.

Set the house rules before anyone arrives

Decide together (or with your partner / support person) what feels workable. Write it once and reuse it in messages:

  • Timing: Prefer late morning or early afternoon over late evenings.
  • Length: 20–40 minutes is enough for most first visits.
  • Who: Immediate family first; wider friends in week two or later.
  • Holding: Optional — you may ask guests to admire without passing the baby.
  • Illness: Anyone with a cough, fever, sore throat, or stomach bug waits.

Send the note before people ask. Clarity feels kinder than last-minute cancellations.

What to ask of visitors

  • Wash hands on arrival; skip strong perfume.
  • No kissing the baby’s face or hands in the early weeks.
  • Leave outdoor shoes at the door if that is your home norm.
  • Bring a meal, groceries, or laundry help — not only gifts that need arranging.
  • Follow the parent’s lead on photos and social posts.
  • Exit cheerfully when the visit window ends, even if the baby is “being good.”

If someone pushes back, a simple line works: “We’re keeping things quiet for two weeks so we can rest and feed. We’ll invite you properly after that.”

Managing energy in the room

Babies often show overload with hiccups, turning away, fussing, or a sudden cry after too much passing-around. Parents may feel the same as tension under a smile. Use these moves:

  1. Keep the lights softer and the TV off during visits.
  2. Limit the number of people in the room with the baby.
  3. Offer a seat and a drink; don’t host a full tea service if you’re exhausted.
  4. If feeding is due, ask guests to wait in another room or step out briefly.
  5. End early if anyone looks unwell — including you.

When remote love is better

Not every relationship needs a physical visit in week one. Voice notes, short video calls, and dropped-off food at the door can feel just as caring. For relatives who live far away, a scheduled call after a feed often lands better than an open-ended “drop by anytime.”

Protecting feeding and recovery

Feeding establishment (breast, bottle, or mixed) needs calm repetition. Long visits that delay feeds or leave parents performing hospitality can quietly undermine that. Recovery after birth — stitches, caesarean healing, blood loss, mood swings — also needs rest that guests cannot see.

  • Keep water and snacks within reach so you don’t “host” while depleted.
  • Accept that the living room may look unfinished; prioritise sleep over tidying for guests.
  • If you have a helper at home, let them gatekeep the door and the clock.

Contact your paediatrician or urgent care if the baby has trouble breathing, is hard to wake for feeds, has a fever, fewer wet nappies than expected, or something simply feels wrong. Parental instinct counts — especially in the first fortnight.

Sample message you can copy

“We’re so glad you want to meet the baby. For the first two weeks we’re keeping visits short (about half an hour), one household at a time, and only when everyone is well. Hand washing, no face kisses, and we’re skipping hold-passes for now. Dropping a meal at the door is the kindest gift. We’ll open the door wider soon — thank you for understanding.”

Gentle takeaways

  • Rest and feeding outrank entertaining in week one and two.
  • Short, planned visits beat surprise open houses.
  • Clear rules reduce conflict; kind guests follow them gladly.
  • Help that lightens the load (food, errands) is better than long chat.
  • Widen the circle when you feel ready — not on someone else’s timeline.
